Consider a uniform solid hemisphere resting on a horizontal plane. 

The centre of mass is directly above the point of contact with the plane. So the the object is in in equilibrium. 

![[Pasted image 20260220100620.png]]


Now consider a tipping the hemisphere to one side and releasing it. 

The centre of mass is now the left of the point of contact, so the weight produces an anti-clockwise moment to return the object to its equilibrium position. 

The solid hemisphere is stale and will return to its equilibrium position if disturbed.

![[Pasted image 20260220100813.png]]

In order to topple and and not return to equilibrium, the hemisphere must be titled enough so that the centre of mass passes over the circumference of the plane face. 

It will topple, landing on its plane face.
![[Pasted image 20260220100908.png]]

You can maintain the hemisphere in a titled position by attaching a mass to oppose the moment from the weight. 

Here a mass $M$ has been attached to a hemisphere of mass $m$. 

The hemisphere is in equilibrium if $mgd_{1}=mgd_{2}$. This is due to clockwise and counterclockwise moments balancing to prevent any rotational movement.
![[Pasted image 20260220101031.png]]


--- 

Hence a solid has been made from a cone and a hemisphere such that the centre of mass is at the centre of the hemisphere. 
![[Pasted image 20260220102622.png]]
As usual, the object is stable with the centre of mass above the point of contact with the plane.

Because the centre of mass is at the centre of rotation, it will always remain above the point of contact as long as we do go past the hemispherical point. 

Therefore, the object is always in equilibrium at any titled position.
